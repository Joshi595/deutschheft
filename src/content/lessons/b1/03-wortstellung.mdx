By now you know the big rules of German word order: the verb in second position, the second verb part at the end, the verb at the end of a subordinate clause. At B1 your sentences get longer, and a new question appears: in what order do all the **other** parts go – the time, the place, the objects, *nicht*? German is flexible here, but not random. This chapter shows the normal order, and how to change it on purpose.

## The idea

Think of a German main clause as having **fields**:

| Position 1 | Verb 1 | Middle field | Verb 2 | (After) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ich | **habe** | gestern wegen des Regens mit dem Bus meine Mutter im Krankenhaus | **besucht**. | |
| Gestern | **musste** | ich leider sehr lange auf den Bus | **warten**, | weil es regnete. |

The two verb parts form a **bracket** (*Satzklammer*). Almost everything goes **inside** the bracket, in the middle field. Only one element goes in front of the conjugated verb, and long subordinate clauses usually come after the bracket.

Inside the middle field, there is a typical order:

1. **pronouns** (short words come first)
2. **time** – *wann?*
3. **reason** – *warum?*
4. **manner** – *wie?*
5. **place** – *wo? wohin?*

The memory aid is **TeKaMoLo**: **te**mporal, **ka**usal, **mo**dal, **lo**kal.

## Step by step

<Step n="1" title="Position 1: one element, any element">

Any single part can go in front of the verb: the subject, a time, a place, an object, even a whole subordinate clause. Whatever is there is **emphasised** or links to what came before. The subject then moves behind the verb.

<Example de="**Ich** fahre morgen mit dem Zug nach Wien." en="I am going to Vienna by train tomorrow. (neutral)" />
<Example de="**Morgen** fahre ich mit dem Zug nach Wien." en="Tomorrow I am going to Vienna by train. (the time is the topic)" />
<Example de="**Nach Wien** fahre ich mit dem Zug, nicht nach Graz." en="It is to Vienna that I am going by train, not to Graz. (contrast)" />

Only **one** element: *Morgen mit dem Zug fahre ich* is wrong.

</Step>

<Step n="2" title="Time – reason – manner – place">

<Example de="Ich fahre **morgen** **wegen eines Termins** **mit dem Zug** **nach Wien**." en="I am going to Vienna by train tomorrow because of an appointment." />
<Example de="Sie ist **gestern** **wegen der Kälte** **schnell** **nach Hause** gegangen." en="Yesterday she went home quickly because of the cold." />

This is the **usual** order, not an iron law. Moving one part to the end of the middle field gives it more weight. But if you are unsure, TeKaMoLo always sounds natural. The most important part of it: **time before place**. *Ich gehe heute ins Kino*, never *Ich gehe ins Kino heute*.

</Step>

<Step n="3" title="Objects and pronouns">

From A2 you know the rule for two objects. At B1 it fits into the bigger picture.

- **Pronouns** come right at the start of the middle field, before time expressions.
- Two **nouns**: dative before accusative.
- Two **pronouns**: accusative before dative.

<Example de="Ich habe **ihr** gestern **das Buch** gegeben." en="I gave her the book yesterday." />
<Example de="Ich habe **es ihr** gestern gegeben." en="I gave it to her yesterday." />
<Example de="Gestern hat **sich** mein Bruder ein neues Auto gekauft." en="Yesterday my brother bought himself a new car." />

Reflexive pronouns (*sich, mich, dir*) also like to be early: right after the verb, or right after a pronoun subject.

</Step>

<Step n="4" title="nicht for the whole sentence">

When *nicht* negates the **whole statement**, it goes **as far back as possible**: after the objects and time expressions, but before the second verb part and before the place or manner that is closely tied to the verb.

<Example de="Ich kenne den Mann **nicht**." en="I do not know the man." />
<Example de="Ich habe den Mann gestern **nicht** gesehen." en="I did not see the man yesterday." />
<Example de="Er fährt morgen **nicht** nach Wien." en="He is not going to Vienna tomorrow." />

*nicht* stands before: the second verb part, a direction or place (*nach Wien, ins Kino*), predicate adjectives and nouns (*nicht müde, nicht Lehrer*), and verbs with a preposition (*nicht auf dich warten*).

</Step>

<Step n="5" title="nicht for one part only">

To negate just **one element**, put *nicht* directly **in front of it**, and usually add a correction with **sondern**.

<Example de="Ich fahre **nicht morgen**, **sondern** übermorgen nach Wien." en="I am not going to Vienna tomorrow but the day after." />
<Example de="**Nicht ich** habe das gesagt, **sondern** mein Chef." en="It was not me who said that, but my boss." />

*sondern* (but rather) follows a negation and replaces the negated part. *aber* (but) just adds a contrast.

</Step>

<Step n="6" title="After the bracket">

Some things normally come **after** the second verb part:

- subordinate clauses: *Ich habe ihn gefragt, **ob er kommt**.*
- comparisons with *als* and *wie*: *Er ist schneller gelaufen **als ich**.*
- in speech, afterthoughts: *Ich habe ihn gesehen, **gestern im Park**.*

<Example de="Wir müssen morgen früh aufstehen, **weil der Zug um sechs fährt**." en="We have to get up early tomorrow because the train leaves at six." />

Putting a long *weil* clause inside the bracket is grammatical but very hard to follow. Put it after.

</Step>

## Compared with English

<Compare en="I go to the cinema **today**." de="Ich gehe **heute** ins Kino." note="English puts time last; German puts time before place." />

<Compare en="I **did not see** him yesterday." de="Ich habe ihn gestern **nicht** gesehen." note="English puts not right after the auxiliary. German pushes nicht far back, just before the participle." />

<Compare en="not tomorrow **but** the day after" de="nicht morgen, **sondern** übermorgen" note="After a negation, 'but' meaning 'instead' is sondern, not aber." />

<Compare en="**Tomorrow** I go to Vienna." de="**Morgen** fahre **ich** nach Wien." note="In English the subject stays before the verb. In German the verb stays second, so the subject moves after it." />

## Typical mistakes

<Mistake wrong="Ich gehe ins Kino **heute**." right="Ich gehe **heute** ins Kino." why="Time comes before place." />

<Mistake wrong="Ich habe **nicht** ihn gesehen." right="Ich habe ihn **nicht** gesehen." why="For the whole sentence, nicht goes after the object, before the participle." />

<Mistake wrong="Morgen **ich fahre** nach Wien." right="Morgen **fahre ich** nach Wien." why="Only one element before the verb; the subject moves behind it." />

<Mistake wrong="Ich fahre nicht morgen, **aber** übermorgen." right="Ich fahre nicht morgen, **sondern** übermorgen." why="After a negation, the correction uses sondern." />

<Mistake wrong="Ich habe gestern **das Buch ihr** gegeben." right="Ich habe **ihr** gestern **das Buch** gegeben." why="Pronouns come early in the middle field, before nouns and time." />

## Summary

<Summary>

| Pos. 1 | Verb 1 | Middle field | Verb 2 | After |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| one element | conjugated verb | pronouns → time → reason → manner → place | participle / infinitive / prefix | clauses, comparisons |

**TeKaMoLo:** temporal, kausal, modal, lokal. At least: **time before place**.

**Objects:** pronouns first · noun dative before noun accusative · pronoun accusative before pronoun dative.

**nicht (whole sentence):** as late as possible, before verb 2, direction, adjective. **nicht (one part):** directly before it, + sondern.

</Summary>
